# Podcast notes: how the best teams get a product to people

For the founders' meeting on Sept 26. Source: Lenny's Podcast, "What world-class GTM looks like in
2026" with Jeanne DeWitt Grosser (YouTube RmnWHz8HD74, published Nov 30, 2025, 1 hr 26 min).

"Go-to-market" in this file just means how you get a product into people's hands: finding them,
pitching them, signing them up, and keeping them.

---

## 1. Who the guest is, and the big idea

**Guest:** Jeanne DeWitt Grosser, COO of Vercel (the company that hosts connectvibe.app). Before that
she spent nine years at Stripe, where she built its early sales team from scratch. She started her
career at Google, on Gmail.

**The big idea, in three sentences.** When lots of companies build similar products, the way you bring
yours to people (every first meeting, every follow-up) becomes what sets you apart, so design it as
carefully as the product itself. Most people say yes to avoid a problem or a risk, not to chase a
dream, so good sellers ask lots of questions, find the pain, and do less than half the talking. The
teams that win treat selling like engineering: write the process down, measure it, find what's broken
every week and fix it, and pass what customers say straight back to the people building the product.

**Why this fits Vibe better than it looks.** The episode is about selling software to companies, and
Vibe is a student social app. But Vibe's plan starts with clubs. Getting 10 clubs to say yes, build a
page and post events is a sales job, and club officers are the buyers. Most lessons below are about
that job. Where the episode doesn't fit (students, the social side), Part 4 says so.

## 2. The lessons

Weeks used below: **Wk 1** = Sep 28–Oct 4 · **Wk 2** = Oct 5–11 · **Wk 3** = Oct 12–18 ·
**Wk 4** = Oct 19–25 · **Wk 5** = Oct 26–31.

### Lesson 1. Pitch the problem you fix, not the dream
- **What she said:** She shared a rough number: about 4 in 5 buyers act to avoid pain or risk, and only
  1 in 5 to gain something new. Talk about the exciting future mostly lands with other founders.
  Everyone else is trying not to fail.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** "Replace Instagram" and "customize your profile" are the dream. A club
  president doesn't want a new social network. They worry about empty events, freshmen who never hear
  about them, posts nobody sees, and everything getting lost when officers graduate. Moving the club to
  a new app is also a risk for *them*: if it flops, members blame the officer.
- **The move:** Rewrite the club pitch as 3 problems Vibe fixes, plus how we take the risk off the
  officer: it's free, we build the page with them in the same visit, we load their events, and nobody
  has to quit Instagram on day one. Try two versions in the first demos; keep the one that gets more yeses.
- **Owner:** Rylan writes it; Franky checks every product claim. **When:** Wk 1, before the first demo.

### Lesson 2. Ask before you show
- **What she said:** Founders hear one hint of a problem and jump straight to "we can fix that." Great
  sellers do less than half the talking. They keep asking "why?", and when a customer asks a question,
  they often ask one back before answering.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** All three of us love the app, and a demo day can easily become a
  30-minute feature tour. We'd leave knowing nothing new about the club. We also don't know yet which
  club problems are real on the Indy campus. Right now we're guessing.
- **The move:** Every demo day opens with 10 minutes of questions before the phone comes out. Use the
  same five each time, e.g.: How do members hear about events now? What went wrong at your last event?
  Who decides which apps the club uses? What happens to your accounts when officers graduate? Why?
  Write the answers in a shared sheet after every visit.
- **Owner:** Rylan (runs every demo). **When:** from the first demo in Wk 1.

### Lesson 3. Make the first meeting worth it, even if they say no
- **What she said:** At Stripe, the first sales meeting wasn't a quiz. It was a whiteboard session where
  the customer drew how their own payments worked. Stripe learned a lot, and the customer left with a
  drawing of their setup they'd never made before. She believes how it *feels* to be sold to becomes
  the difference when products look alike. She also tries to give something useful at every contact,
  because people who say no often come back years later.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** To a club, Vibe looks a lot like things they already use: Instagram,
  GroupMe, BoilerLink, The Spot. How our meeting feels is one of the few things that sets us apart today.
  And a club that says "not now" in October may say yes in January, when new officers take over.
- **The move:** Turn demo day into a "plan your semester" session. On a whiteboard or paper, lay out the
  club's remaining fall events and where each one gets announced. The club keeps that plan either way.
  If they say yes, the page gets built and those events go up right there (as already planned). If they
  say no, write down why and when to come back.
- **Owner:** Rylan runs it. Franky makes sure page setup plus events takes under 15 minutes on a phone
  (a target, not a measured number). **When:** Wk 1–2.

### Lesson 4. Bring them a fact they don't have
- **What she said:** Vercel starts conversations with something useful: how fast a company's website
  is, compared with similar companies. Even if they don't buy, they learned something, and they start
  seeing Vercel as worth listening to.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** Rylan is already doing student interviews. That gives us something no
  club officer has: what Indy students say about how they find clubs and events. That's a much better
  opener than "check out our app." After launch, Vibe can also show each club how its events did.
- **The move:** Do 20 to 30 quick student interviews in Wk 1 with the same 3 questions (How do you hear
  about club events? What made you skip one? What would get you to join a new club?). Turn the answers
  into 3 short facts and open every demo with them. After launch, show each club a simple "how your
  events did" number (how many saw it, how many RSVP'd).
- **Owner:** Rylan (interviews and facts); Franky (the after-launch club numbers). **When:** Wk 1 for
  interviews; the club numbers after launch.

### Lesson 5. Pick the first clubs on purpose, using 3 traits, and make sure all three of us know who they are
- **What she said:** Split the market by what changes how people decide. More than about 3 traits is
  too much for a small team; a handful of people can't cover five different groups. In her first 30 days
  at Vercel she looked at where they were winning and found patterns nobody expected. She also teaches
  this to every new hire in their first week, so the people building the product know who each feature
  is for.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** We need 10 clubs by a deadline and have 1 (and 0 at Purdue). If the 10
  are all different kinds of clubs, we won't learn what works, and every demo starts from scratch. Also,
  Franky picks what to build every day; he needs the same picture of the first customer that Rylan has.
- **The move:** Rylan's club research list gets 3 yes/no columns, for example: (a) holds an event at
  least every two weeks, (b) is actively recruiting new members this fall, (c) already posts on
  Instagram, so it feels the "nobody sees our posts" problem. Go after clubs with 3 yeses first, and
  make sure at least 3 of the 10 are Purdue clubs. At the Sept 26 meeting, agree on one sentence that
  describes our first customer and put it at the top of the roadmap.
- **Owner:** Rylan (the list); all three (the one sentence). **When:** Sep 26 meeting and Wk 1.

### Lesson 6. Write the playbook down after a few tries
- **What she said:** Many founders think selling is just showing up and talking. It's a skill with
  parts: the questions you ask, how you handle pushback, what you show. Nobody else can take it over,
  or turn it into a tool, until someone writes down what works.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** If this works, more people than Rylan will pitch clubs: officers who tell
  other clubs, campus reps, James. Right now the pitch lives in our heads. And "we already use GroupMe
  and Instagram" will come up in almost every meeting; the answer should be the same every time.
- **The move:** After the first 3 demo days, write a one-page club playbook: the 5 questions, the
  3-problem pitch, the 5 most common pushbacks with our best answer to each, and the steps to set up a
  page. Update it every Friday.
- **Owner:** Rylan. **When:** end of Wk 2.

### Lesson 7. Hand the work over for real, but stay in the room
- **What she said:** When founders hand off selling, two things go wrong. Either they don't pass on
  what they know (their questions, their answers, their materials), or they vanish completely. The
  founder still needs to hear customers, because that's where the next product decisions come from.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** Franky is handing the campus to Rylan while he builds the store apps.
  That's the right split. But if Franky never hears a club officer, he'll build from guesses, and Rylan
  will be stuck answering product questions alone.
- **The move:** Franky puts everything he knows about the product and privacy in writing (see
  Lesson 8), then goes to 1 demo a week as a listener, not the lead. Rylan runs the meeting.
- **Owner:** Franky (hand-off and weekly visit); Rylan (runs the meetings). **When:** Wk 1 through Wk 5.

### Lesson 8. Whoever pitches must know the product cold
- **What she said:** Her test for her sales team: put a seller in front of 10 engineers and see how long
  it takes them to notice the seller isn't a product person. In her words, "it should take them 10
  minutes to figure out you aren't a product manager" (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser). Knowing the product
  deeply earns respect from the builders and trust from customers.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** Club officers will ask hard questions. Who can see our member list? Can
  someone fake being a student? What if someone posts something awful? Can we remove a follower? Vibe
  has good answers (a verified school email to post, counts public but names private, the report
  button and our moderation desk, officers can remove followers). Right now only Franky knows them well.
- **The move:** Franky runs a 45-minute walkthrough and hands over a one-page "tough questions" sheet.
  Then Rylan and James each give Franky a 5-minute demo as if he were a doubtful club president. Nobody
  leads a demo day until they've done this once.
- **Owner:** Franky runs it; Rylan and James pass it. **When:** Wk 1.

### Lesson 9. What customers say is product research, so log it and use a "3 times" rule
- **What she said:** The best sales teams are part money-makers, part researchers: a sales team talks
  to more customers in a week than the product people do. The hard part is telling a real gap from a
  one-off complaint. Is this pushback we should answer, or something we should build? Her test: have
  we heard it on the last three calls?
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** In October, Rylan will talk to far more students and officers than Franky
  will. And Franky's build list is already full. He can't chase every idea one club mentions.
- **The move:** One shared "what we heard" sheet: date, club, what they said, how many times we've heard
  it. A request goes onto Franky's build list only after 3 different clubs or students bring it up.
  Anything else becomes an answer in the playbook. Look at it together every Friday.
- **Owner:** Rylan logs; Franky decides what gets built. **When:** from Wk 1.

### Lesson 10. Treat a lost club like a bug, and look for the real reason
- **What she said:** Vercel ran an AI tool over its biggest lost deals. The salesperson said a big one
  was lost on price. The real story: they never reached the person who makes the decision, and never
  showed what it was worth. Now they review losses every week and fix the gaps the way engineers fix
  bugs, with new answers, a new demo, or new materials by the next week.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** When a club says "we're fine with Instagram," that may not be the real
  reason. Maybe the president wasn't in the room. Maybe the social chair runs the accounts. Maybe we
  never showed what the club gets. If we accept the first reason, we'll keep losing the same way.
- **The move:** After every no or maybe, write 3 lines: who was in the room, the reason they gave, and
  what we think the real reason was. Every Friday, hold a 20-minute huddle: pick the top one or two
  "bugs" in how we pitch and fix them by next Friday.
- **Owner:** all three (Rylan brings the notes, James keeps time). **When:** Fridays Oct 2, 9, 16, 23, 30.

### Lesson 11. Get to a clear yes or no, fast
- **What she said:** She passed on a saying from a sales trainer: a yes and a no are both useful because
  they tell you something, while a maybe is what really hurts you. Separately, her team's reply tool
  needed fewer back-and-forths than people did, just because it answered right away instead of leaving
  people waiting overnight.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** We need 10 clubs, and a list full of maybes feels like progress but
  isn't. Officers text back at midnight; if we answer three days later, the moment is gone.
- **The move:** Every club we're talking to gets a decision date (about a week after the demo). A maybe
  past its date counts as a no and moves to a "come back in January" list, with the reason. Every club
  message gets an answer within 24 hours.
- **Owner:** Rylan (dates and replies); James covers the vibe.llc inbox as backup. **When:** from Wk 1.

### Lesson 12. Keep score from day one
- **What she said:** She tells founders to bring in someone who tracks the numbers on selling earlier
  than feels necessary, because data and a clear process show a founder what's working. She also likes
  mixing backgrounds (sales people with numbers people) because each learns from the other.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** Right now our numbers come from one person pulling counts by hand (on
  Sept 25: 20 accounts, 1 real club, 0 upcoming events). James handles money and paperwork. Owning the
  scoreboard is a natural fit, and it pairs his numbers work with Rylan's people work.
- **The move:** James owns a scoreboard every Friday: clubs researched, contacted, demo done, said yes,
  page live, first event posted, reached 5 followers. Rylan feeds the first four. Franky sends the
  app-side counts (numbers only, no names, since only Franky can open the database). James also goes
  to at least one demo day in October, so the numbers have faces.
- **Owner:** James. **When:** first scoreboard Fri Oct 2, then every Friday.

### Lesson 13. Give clubs a "how to do it well" guide, not just a help page
- **What she said:** Good companies write help pages and stop there. Customers want more: the best way
  to set it up for someone like them. Stripe had seen every big marketplace, so new marketplaces wanted
  Stripe's recipe, not general instructions.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** A club with a blank page and no idea what to post will go quiet, and quiet
  clubs make the campus look empty on Oct 31. Once a few clubs are live, we'll know what a good page
  looks like.
- **The move:** Write a one-page club starter kit: what to post the first week, how to set up events,
  how to reach the first 5 followers (when the follower count starts to show), plus 2 good examples.
  Hand it over at every signing.
- **Owner:** Rylan writes it; Franky adds screenshots. **When:** Wk 3.

### Lesson 14. Let tools do the repeat work, but only after a person has done it well
- **What she said:** At Stripe she got 4 outreach people where most companies would hire 30, so the team
  had to get clever. At Vercel, one engineer, working on it part-time, built an AI helper in six weeks
  that now does the first replies to new leads with one person checking it, work that used to take 10.
  Her rules: start with the simplest, most repeatable task; first watch your best person do it; a human
  reviews and sends everything; track the same numbers you'd track for a person.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** Three of us, 30-plus clubs to reach, and Franky already builds with AI
  helpers every day. It's tempting to automate outreach now. But we don't have a message that works
  yet, and a clumsy auto-message to a club president could burn that club for the year.
- **The move:** Rylan sends the first 10 to 15 club messages by hand and notes which get replies. Then,
  only if Rylan wants it, Franky sets up a helper that drafts a personal first message for each club
  from Rylan's research sheet. Rylan reads, edits and sends every one himself.
- **Owner:** Rylan first; Franky builds the helper only on Rylan's ask. **When:** Wk 2 at the earliest.

### Lesson 15. Treat pricing as part of the product (after launch)
- **What she said:** Free plans often get added out of habit, not as a real decision. Stripe dropped a
  free trial on one product because once customers set it up, they stayed anyway. At Vercel, half the
  buyers of the big-company plan turned out to be startups, so they moved those features into a plan
  anyone could buy online, and sales went up. Watch who buys what, then change it.
- **Why it matters for Vibe:** Vibe+ is $3.99 a month for students and switched off until the bank
  account and Terms are ready. Students don't have much money; clubs have budgets. We don't know yet
  who will pay, or for what.
- **The move:** Don't treat the Vibe+ bundle as final. After launch, watch which paid features students
  tap on most (like "who viewed your profile"). In demo days, ask officers one question: would the club
  ever pay for anything on Vibe, and what? No selling, just asking.
- **Owner:** Franky (the after-launch numbers); Rylan (the one question). **When:** the question from
  Wk 1; decisions after launch.

## 3. Principles we could adopt ("how we work")

1. **One path, one name on every step.** Getting a club from "never heard of Vibe" to "posts every
   week" is one path, not three separate to-do lists. Every step has one of our names next to it.
2. **Ask first, pitch second.** In any meeting with a club or a student, we ask questions before we
   show anything, and they talk more than we do.
3. **Every no teaches us something, and maybes get a date.** We write down the real reason for every
   no, and we don't let maybes sit around looking like progress.
4. **A broken pitch is a bug.** Every Friday we pick what isn't working in how we sign clubs and fix it
   by the next Friday, the same way Franky fixes code.
5. **If it isn't written down, only one person can do it.** The pitch, the answers, the scoreboard and
   the product facts live on paper, so any of us can pick them up.

## 4. Where the episode pushes back on Vibe's plan

Being honest: some of this argues against what we're doing now.

1. **"Replace Instagram" is a dream pitch.** The episode says most people act to avoid a problem, not
   to chase something new. As our own goal, "replace Instagram" is fine. As the line we say to a club
   president, it asks them to take a big risk. Pitch the problems we fix; replacing Instagram is what
   happens over time if we're right.
2. **Franky's October time may be in the wrong place.** The episode's core claim is that when products
   look alike, how you bring them to people matters as much as the product. Today Franky's time goes
   to store paperwork and the native apps, while the live numbers show 1 real club, 0 upcoming events
   and 0 Purdue students. The store apps matter too (students expect to find us in the App Store). But
   the first 3 to 5 club meetings shape the pitch everything else rests on, and the episode says the
   founder shouldn't be missing from them. One demo a week (Lesson 7) is the minimum.
3. **We may be aiming at too many kinds of clubs at once.** A small team can handle about 3 traits, not
   five different groups. "Any club that says yes" (Greek life, faith, cultural, academic, IU and
   Purdue) spreads 10 clubs so thin that no pattern shows up. Most of the first 10 should look alike
   (Lesson 5), while still including Purdue clubs because the campus is shared.
4. **Don't automate before it works by hand.** We lean on AI helpers for almost everything. The
   episode is clear that tools come after a person has a process that works, not before (Lesson 14).
5. **The episode says almost nothing about the student side.** It's about companies buying software.
   A social app lives or dies on whether your friends are on it, and nothing here covers that. So these
   lessons apply to signing clubs, not to getting students to join and stay. Don't run a sales process
   on students; the clubs and their events are what bring students in.
6. **A student subscription is an untested guess.** The episode says free-versus-paid choices are often
   made out of habit, and that you should watch who actually pays and move features to match
   (Lesson 15). That hints clubs, not students, may be the ones who pay. That's our reading, not
   something she said about Vibe.

**What backs the plan:** She says growth where people sign up on their own eventually hits a ceiling,
and companies stall when they add hands-on selling too late. Vibe does both from the start: students
sign up on their own, and clubs get signed one by one, in person. The episode supports doing clubs first.
